### Patterns across all four drafts

- **Hedging gets in the way.** Nearly every source is followed by a paragraph explaining what it *doesn't* prove ("This study concerns…", "The experiment uses an earlier model…", "This is an illustrative case."). That is honest, but stacked up it drains momentum. Fold most caveats into one clause inside the sentence that cites the source, and keep standalone caveat paragraphs only where a reader would otherwise overclaim.
- **Too many abstract nouns.** Phrases like "institutional route", "operational consequence" and "the arrangement" pile up. Each section needs one person or object the reader can picture.
- **The sentence rhythm is the same throughout.** Short declaratives in threes ("Both interrupt the sequence. Both make the next step take longer.") work as punctuation. Used in almost every paragraph, they turn into a tic. Vary them.
- **Similar endings.** Each piece closes on a single aphoristic line, and that's good. Check that the paragraph before it doesn't already make the same point.
- **Source hygiene.** Transcript citations say "supplied transcript". For publication, replace that with the episode title and date, and drop the internal phrasing.

## 1. The Assistant That Saves You Into Spending

**Why it ranks first:** It has the best opening of the set ("The assistant finds the better deal. The holiday costs more."). There's a recurring human anchor (the Scottish traveller), and the $2,000 → $2,200 worked example makes the thesis instantly testable. The tone is light without being glib, and the ending ("The money left in the account needs an advocate too") lands.

**Improvements:**
1. **Cut about 15%.** "The conversion report" and "The sponsored bargain" each spend two paragraphs on caveats about observational data. Merge the Amazon and Adobe caveats into one paragraph.
2. **Drop the Rufus → Alexa for Shopping renaming aside** from the body text. It's trivia that breaks flow, and the bibliography can carry it.
3. **The Amazon page is cited twice** (purchase stat and auto-buy). That's fine, but name it the same way both times so readers don't think it's two sources.
4. **The "personal preference" section is the freshest material.** The 79% first-person-recommendation finding is striking, so consider pulling it up earlier, perhaps straight after "The receipt", to set up the trust problem before the commerce sections.
5. **The $799 laptop line is the most memorable example in the piece.** Give it its own short paragraph.
6. **"The standing instruction" ends in a list of design fixes.** Trim it to the one strongest idea (expiring instructions or a monthly allowance), or it reads like a spec.
7. **Minor:** "Fold them together and the upgraded plan becomes the default picture…" is excellent. More sentences like it, fewer like "The available evidence supports concern about increased purchasing."

**Remaining work before publishing:** a line edit and a check that every link still resolves. No new reporting is needed.

## 2. The Welfare State After Work

**Why it ranks second:** It has the most quotable lines of the batch: "The tenant becomes the forwarding address." "The expensive items have a tiresome habit of occupying land." "turns an opportunity into a compulsory audition." The household arithmetic is checked and correct ($900 × 0.8 = $720 against $500 income, $220 short). It treats the evidence fairly in both directions (NBER trial versus Kenya transfers).

**Improvements:**
1. **Length.** It's the longest piece. "The tax base" and "Permission to receive" could be merged and cut by about a third. The Robodebt material is strong, but it opens a second topic (automated administration) late in the piece.
2. **The opening needs a person.** "The worker gets a free AI tutor and loses the job" is good, but the worker then disappears. Bring them back in "The customer problem" (the overdue-electricity-bill line is the right instinct) and again at the close.
3. **The rent-seeker quote is your best collision.** Make sure the Critical Path timestamp (`t=664s` in the body, 10:04 / `t=604s` in the bibliography) matches. Right now the body and bibliography disagree.
4. **The NBER paragraph** packs four stats into three sentences. Lead with the one that matters ("people worked about one to two hours less a week"), then add the rest.
5. **The Alaska section** spends three paragraphs on limits. Keep one.
6. **The ending works,** but "The test of a proposed settlement…" through "…hold to its promise" is three paragraphs making the same point before the final line. Cut to one.

**Remaining work before publishing:** structural trimming (about 20%), fixing the timestamp, and a line edit.

## 3. The Right to Be Friction

**Why it ranks third:** It has the best core idea and the sharpest definition ("the right to interrupt a process for a reason that the process has yet to recognize"). The Toyota andon, Columbia and override-experiment material is well chosen. But it reads most like a policy paper. The memo itself flags that the central reporting is missing: *a concrete case where an employee's challenge changed an AI rollout, and what happened to their performance review.* Without that, the benefits-officer case is hypothetical, and the draft says so ("This is an illustrative case").

**Improvements:**
1. **Get one real case,** even a brief one. A real case would carry more weight than any other change to the piece.
2. **The opening is strong but clumsily built.** The staircase image is attributed in sentence two and then left behind. Keep the image, put Robinson's quoted words straight after it, and bring the "negative-value employee" in by paragraph 3, not paragraph 3 plus a caveat about predictions. Also cut "unsupported by the institutional validation he names". It's opaque.
3. **Sections run long and abstract.** "The designated human" and "The warning" each have about two paragraphs that restate the section's thesis. Trim them.
4. **The accommodation section is the weakest link.** It's interesting, but it switches legal frameworks and then admits it partly undercuts the Toyota analogy. Either commit to it as the piece's turn ("not all friction pays for itself") and say so up front, or cut it to one paragraph.
5. **The California AB 2575 section** is well sourced, but its third and fourth paragraphs relitigate the veto reasoning. Keep it to: what the bill tried, why it was vetoed, the one remaining gap.
6. **The "The pause" checklist** (request, decision, route, record) is the piece's practical payoff. Consider setting it as a short list so readers can lift it.
7. **The penultimate paragraph** returns to the staircase researcher, which is good. "Someone has to be allowed to inconvenience the machine" is a great last line, so protect it.

**Remaining work before publishing:** one reported case, a cut of about 20%, and an opening rework.

## 4. The Social Life of a Frozen Model

**Why it ranks fourth:** The idea is clever: weights are frozen, but institutions accumulate memory. Hutchins' cockpit is an inspired comparison. But the piece is the most abstract of the four, and its most vivid material (METR: 1,200 agents, 70,000 messages, about 700 in an attack) doesn't arrive until the fourth section.

**Improvements:**
1. **Move the board up.** Open with the METR incident (a fresh agent finds a message board, not a neutral folder), then bring in Voss. The current neutral "agent opens a folder" opening undersells it.
2. **Cut the explanatory throat-clearing** in "The handover". The weights explainer and the continual-learning survey can shrink to two sentences each.
3. **"We seldom demand…"** is the one use of first-person-plural "we" in the drafts. Check whether that fits your voice, or rephrase it ("Organizations seldom demand…").
4. **"Inherited truth" and "Disagreement in the archive" are the original contribution.** They show summaries flattening uncertainty and dissent disappearing from the record. Give them more room and give "The party and the convention" less. The naming-game study takes two paragraphs to disclaim.
5. **Join it to Friction.** "Disagreement in the archive" is the agent-world version of the right to be friction. A one-line cross-reference, or publishing the two pieces close together, strengthens both.
6. **Too many hedges per paragraph** in "The board". The operational-versus-experiential distinction is made three times. Once is enough.
7. **The ending** ("Whoever governs that folder helps decide what the next arrival inherits") is good. The paragraph before it on ownership and export feels like a new topic. Cut it or move it.

**Remaining work before publishing:** restructure (reorder sections), a cut of about 25%, and a stronger opening.
