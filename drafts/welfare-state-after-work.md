# The Welfare State After Work

The worker gets a free AI tutor and loses the job. The tutor can explain the economics of abundance. The landlord still wants the money.

There is a peculiar gap in the promise of a world where machines do more of the work. Production becomes cheaper. Human effort becomes less necessary. Somewhere between those developments, everyone is supposed to become richer.

Nobody has yet described the bank transfer connecting them.

In a [Critical Path discussion of post-labor economics](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ItBq5rZSeh0&t=664s), a speaker questions the reassuring claim that technology always creates replacement jobs. Employers often want an outcome; hiring a person is the available route to it. A machine that delivers the outcome more cheaply can remove the reason for hiring. The speaker calls humans “rent seekers” in that arrangement, a description with a certain chill when applied to the people buying groceries with the proceeds.

Elsewhere, in an [All-In discussion of welfare and subsidies](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TBLHdXABwAg&t=3844s), guaranteed support comes under attack for weakening incentives and driving up costs. Later in the same conversation, [the proposed future is a society of makers](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TBLHdXABwAg&t=5361s): people using capable systems to produce something others want to buy.

These are distinct arguments from different speakers. Their collision exposes an unfinished payment arrangement. One future reduces the economic demand for human labor. Another expects people to keep earning their living through useful production. People caught between them receive a remarkable toolkit and a familiar obligation to find a customer.

The future may contain plenty of new jobs. In its [2025 assessment of occupational exposure to generative AI](https://www.ilo.org/resource/article/how-might-generative-ai-impact-different-occupations), the International Labour Organization estimates that around a quarter of global employment has some exposure. Exposure measures potential changes to tasks, and the ILO regards transformation as the likelier outcome for most affected jobs. A forecast of universal unemployment would outrun that evidence.

The question still deserves an answer before universal unemployment arrives. If a society succeeds in producing more with less paid labor, how do people acquire a claim on the output?

## The price of cheap

A fall in prices can make an unchanged income go further. That is a real gain. Someone paying less for the same necessities has more room to live.

The arithmetic changes when the income falls too.

Imagine a household earning $1,000 a month and spending $900 on essentials. Automation halves its income while cutting the cost of those essentials by a fifth. The household now earns $500 and needs $720. Everything in the example is cheaper. The household is short $220.

No prediction hides in those invented numbers. They show why cheaper production and greater personal purchasing power require separate calculations. The first can happen while the second deteriorates.

Digital abundance also arrives unevenly across a household’s expenses. An explanation of a legal document can become extremely cheap while legal representation remains expensive. A tutor can help someone understand physiology while a treatment still requires a staffed clinic. A beautifully generated apartment exists on the screen. The tenancy exists somewhere with a front door.

Housing brings the distinction into focus because desirable locations remain desirable even when software becomes extraordinary. A household needs a place near the people it cares for, or near work that still requires a body. Making another million images of a city does little for its available floor space.

Automation could help build homes and improve medical delivery. Those are plausible benefits worth pursuing. They involve physical capacity and institutions whose behavior cannot be inferred from the falling price of a chatbot response.

The promise of abundance sometimes treats the household budget as though every line item were a digital service awaiting its next discount. The expensive items have a tiresome habit of occupying land. *The Assistant That Saves You Into Spending* runs the same arithmetic at the scale of a single holiday budget.

## The customer problem

The maker future has real appeal. Lower costs can let someone attempt a business that once required serious capital. A capable assistant may help a small operation survive work that previously swallowed its owner’s time. People who struggled to turn an idea into a product may get a usable route through.

Some will earn more. Some new markets will emerge. Neither outcome supplies a general income mechanism for everyone whose old work becomes less valuable.

A tool that helps people produce also helps their competitors produce. When comparable services multiply, buyers gain options and sellers can face pressure on prices. Someone who once sold a scarce skill may discover that the new toolkit is available to everyone bidding for the same contract.

The instruction to become a maker then becomes an instruction to find a remaining scarcity. A personal reputation built within a particular community might help. Ownership of a distribution channel helps rather more.

There is also a customer at the other end of the proposed business. That customer needs purchasing power. A population can become impressively productive while struggling to sell to one another if its reliable incomes shrink. The ease of making something does not determine who can afford it.

Someone starting from savings can test a market for months. Someone starting from an overdue electricity bill needs a buyer before the experiment has time to become educational. The same assistant offers each person technical help, while their financial runway sets very different conditions for using it.

Entrepreneurship remains one possible livelihood. Making it the universal response to displacement turns an opportunity into a compulsory audition.

Existing welfare institutions already recognize that paid work cannot finance every stage of a human life. A pension lets a person draw income after employment ends. Unemployment insurance spreads some risk of losing a job. Such arrangements have different eligibility rules and wide gaps, yet they demonstrate that societies can assign purchasing power through relationships beyond a current wage.

A post-work settlement would need to extend that reasoning into a much more contested circumstance: someone capable of working whose labor attracts insufficient demand.

## A share in the machinery

The owner of a productive asset has a claim that survives a reduction in their personal workload. If the asset keeps generating income, its owner can receive income while doing less work. That arrangement is familiar enough to feature prominently in retirement planning.

For a person relying entirely on wages, the same reduction in required work can threaten the income itself. A productive economy therefore needs an account of who owns its productivity. The distribution of leisure follows the distribution of claims.

Alaska offers an existing, limited example of public ownership supporting personal income. The state created its [Permanent Fund in 1976](https://apfc.org/the-fund/), constitutionally setting aside at least a quarter of specified mineral revenues for investment. Eligible residents can receive an [annual Permanent Fund Dividend](https://pfd.alaska.gov/faq). The payment operates within eligibility rules and a political system that governs the use of earnings. It provides a supplement, with no promise that it will cover a household’s living costs.

Oil revenues and AI profits have different ownership histories, and Alaska’s arrangement cannot simply be copied onto a collection of privately owned technology companies. It demonstrates a narrower possibility: public decisions can convert a source of wealth into an invested asset from which people receive a recurring payment.

An AI-era version would require choices about how the public acquires its stake. Governments could invest public capital on terms that preserve an ownership interest. They could use part of tax revenues to build a diversified fund. Each route needs rules governing who receives the return and who controls the asset.

A fund can lose value. A government can spend its earnings badly. A nominal public stake can become a subsidy for investors if the terms protect private gains while assigning losses to everyone else. Calling something a social wealth fund does little accounting work by itself.

Still, ownership changes the basis of the payment. The recipient holds a claim tied to an institution and its assets. That gives a different footing from waiting for a successful company’s founder to feel generous.

The distinction also changes the moral weather around income. A shareholder receiving a distribution seldom needs to demonstrate a recent burst of personal usefulness. Someone receiving public support may face a recurring examination of whether they deserve to eat while looking for work.

If the machinery makes paid human effort less necessary, that asymmetry becomes harder to defend. Society cannot celebrate the removal of labor from production and indefinitely treat a lack of paid labor as a personal defect.

## The cash test

Unconditional income has enough evidence behind it to make sweeping claims awkward for both enthusiasts and opponents.

Given $1,000 a month for three years, people worked about one to two hours less per week. That is the headline of a [US randomized trial reported in an NBER working paper](https://www.nber.org/papers/w32719), in which 1,000 low-income participants received the payment and a comparison group of 2,000 received $50 a month. The paper’s August 2026 revision also reports a 4.2 percentage point reduction in labor-force participation. Leisure accounts for the largest increase in time use. The researchers find no improvement in employment quality or degree attainment.

That result gives a reason to resist the story in which a guaranteed payment reliably turns everyone into a better-paid entrepreneur. It also gives a scale to the work-incentive objection. People reduced paid work modestly in this setting; the result does not describe a general abandonment of employment.

A three-year trial within an economy organized around wages cannot show how a permanent national payment would function after large-scale automation. Its participants still lived among employers recruiting people and businesses serving wage earners. The financing of a nationwide scheme would introduce effects that this experiment did not test.

It can make the political question clearer, though. If a society aims to reduce the necessity of paid work, some reduction in paid work is compatible with the aim. Treating every hour away from employment as a policy failure would put the promise of liberation on a rather short leash.

That does not remove the problem of funding essential labor. A clinic needs staff even if its patients have incomes independent of employment. A settlement that increases people’s freedom to decline unpleasant work has to address the pay and conditions that make necessary work worth accepting.

Employers could face higher wage demands for jobs people previously accepted through desperation. Consumers or taxpayers might bear some of that cost. Those effects belong in the design of a scheme, alongside its potential benefits.

Cash can buy time. What people do with that time varies. A welfare arrangement should be assessed against the purpose chosen for it, with the cost visible. Promising freedom and then scoring recipients chiefly on how quickly they return to employment creates a particularly cramped definition of success.

## The landlord’s dividend

The objection that subsidies can raise prices deserves more than an impatient dismissal. Where supply is constrained, extra purchasing power can give buyers more money to compete for the same goods. Some of the payment can reach the seller through higher prices.

The OECD makes this concern explicit in its [2026 report on affordable and social housing](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/tackling-the-affordability-gap-through-increased-supply-of-affordable-and-social-housing_60b5a398-en/full-report.html). Demand-side housing support, including incentives for buyers, can push up house prices where supply is constrained. Those programs have specific designs; their effects cannot stand in for every form of cash support. The underlying warning remains relevant to any income proposal that leaves a severe housing shortage untouched.

A government can transfer money to a household and discover that the landlord has acquired a new income stream. The tenant becomes the forwarding address.

That possibility argues for examining the market receiving the money. Building more affordable housing can increase the choices available to tenants. Public provision can give a household access to a service without requiring it to bid against everyone else for a scarce private substitute. Cash and capacity need to be considered together.

The evidence also resists the opposite absolute: that cash support inevitably dissolves into inflation. A [large experiment in rural Kenya](https://emiguel.econ.berkeley.edu/research/general-equilibrium-effects-of-cash-transfers-experimental-evidence-from-kenya/) provided one-time transfers of about $1,000 to more than 10,500 poor households across 653 randomized villages. Recipients gained in consumption and assets. Benefits also spread to other households and firms, with minimal price inflation. The intervention amounted to a large shock relative to the local economy.

That setting differs from a permanent national basic income. It also differs from an urban housing market with tight restrictions on building. The results establish that a large cash injection can expand economic activity without a matching rise in prices, in the circumstances studied.

The policy question concerns the circumstances. Can suppliers respond to demand? Does competition give consumers somewhere else to go? A blanket claim about handouts avoids the market analysis that would make the objection useful.

An AI welfare settlement could fail through generous cash payments combined with neglected physical provision. It could also fail through excellent public services that leave people without money for needs those services do not cover. Choosing the balance requires attention to how people live, down to the invoice.

## The tax base

A government cannot pay a durable income by writing abundance on the revenue side of a budget.

If automation shifts income toward the owners of capital, financing social protection through labor income alone becomes a strained arrangement. Displaced workers need support while the earnings used to fund that support come under pressure. The gains may sit in a different part of the economy, governed by a different set of tax rules.

The [IMF’s fiscal-policy recommendations for generative AI](https://www.imf.org/en/blogs/articles/2024/06/17/fiscal-policy-can-help-broaden-the-gains-of-ai-to-humanity) include stronger taxation of capital income to protect the tax base and counter rising inequality. The authors also recommend broader social protection for people facing disruption. They advise against a specific tax on AI, citing difficulties of implementation and potential damage to investment. Their proposals concern managing uncertain transitions, with no assumption that all employment disappears.

Taxing a particular machine invites a definitional contest over which equipment counts. Taxing profits or capital income addresses established categories, although enforcement remains difficult. The objective should determine the instrument: a reliable public claim on an appropriate share of economic gains, with room for productive investment to continue.

Geography makes that arrangement harder. A country can lose demand for work while the profits associated with replacing it accrue to owners elsewhere. A national dividend depends on the assets that nation owns or the revenues it can collect. A global increase in output does not automatically appear in every treasury.

People outside the wealthy technology centers may receive access to powerful tools without receiving a share of their owners’ income. Cheap access is valuable. It still leaves the question of a livelihood open when the tool competes with what its user used to sell.

Even a generous domestic settlement can preserve this divide. Residents within one jurisdiction receive a dividend; people elsewhere face the new competition with weaker protection. The political constituency for an AI benefit is likely to have a border, however borderless the sales pitch sounds.

There is no simple global collection mechanism waiting to resolve this. International tax cooperation would have to carry some of the argument into practice. Claims that everyone will become richer need to account for people whose governments have little bargaining power over where that wealth is booked.

## Permission to receive

The payment mechanism includes the office, or automated system, deciding who qualifies.

A benefit described as an entitlement can become precarious through the way it is administered. A person misses a notice because a database holds an old address. A household can lose access to money while the institution sorts out which account of its circumstances is correct.

Automation may make administration faster and easier for many recipients. It can also increase the speed at which a disputed decision acquires consequences. Saving money on casework is a poor bargain if people must supply unpaid labor to correct the system’s mistakes.

Australia’s [Robodebt Royal Commission recommendations](https://robodebt.royalcommission.gov.au/publications/report) offer concrete requirements for automated government decisions. The commission calls for a clear route to review and plain-language explanations of how automation works. It recommends publishing business rules and algorithms for independent scrutiny, alongside oversight of automated decision-making. These are recommendations arising from a particular welfare scandal, with relevance well beyond its technology. *The Right to Be Friction* follows the same scheme from inside, through a compliance officer whose warnings were recast as her failure to understand the system.

A future income settlement should make those protections ordinary. Someone needs to be able to learn why a payment stopped and obtain a correction through a route they can use. A proposed guarantee should also specify what happens to essential support while a decision is disputed. The household’s need continues during the institution’s investigation.

This becomes more consequential if paid work provides fewer alternatives. A recipient with an available job may have another route to income, however difficult. A recipient in a region where demand for their work has collapsed depends more heavily on the institution withholding payment.

The state can acquire extraordinary influence over a person who cannot afford to lose its support. So can a private provider if benefits depend on a commercial account. A secure income needs protection against having an unrelated disagreement converted into a survival problem.

Some conditions may be justified by the design of a particular benefit. They should be explicit and open to challenge. A vague promise of universal support can conceal a very particular idea of the acceptable recipient.

The prospect becomes especially absurd when automated production removes a person’s job and automated administration demands repeated evidence that they are searching for its replacement. The citizen performs employability for one machine while another makes the performance economically unnecessary.

## The settlement

The welfare state after work may emerge through extensions to existing institutions rather than a single grand replacement. Broader insurance could help people through displacement. A public asset fund could build a recurring claim on investment returns. Different countries will have different capacities to finance either arrangement.

Universal payments have advantages of simplicity and reach, alongside heavy financing demands. Targeted support can concentrate resources on greater need, while eligibility tests introduce boundaries that people can fall through. Those choices remain material even when software can process applications cheaply.

The test of a proposed settlement is whether a person can understand the claim they hold and rely on it enough to organize a life. The amount matters. So does its relationship to housing costs. A generous nominal payment with uncertain access can leave someone less secure than a smaller payment they know will arrive.

The promise that work will become optional raises the standard. An optional activity can be declined without losing the means to live. A person obliged to sell something each month remains under an economic requirement to work, even when an assistant helps with the selling.

There will be people who want demanding employment and people who want to make things. Their choices can coexist with a durable claim for those whose work attracts little demand. A prosperous society should have room for someone’s contribution to exceed its market price, and for someone’s needs to exceed their current contribution.

A post-work society still owes its people a living.
