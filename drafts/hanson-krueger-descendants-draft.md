# The Children Are Not an Armada

*Robin Hanson, David Krueger, and the question of who gets to call themselves independent*

DRAFT · October 2026

---

Two videos about the end of the world came out in the same season. They feature the same economist, and the thumbnails are the wrong way round.

In the first, a solo interview on *Digital Disruption*, Robin Hanson sits under a title that would make a doomer blush: **"AI Is Probably the End of Humanity."** In the second, he debates David Krueger, a machine-learning researcher trained under Yoshua Bengio. That one asks, more politely, **"Will AI Destroy Humanity?"**, and Hanson plays the sceptic.

You could put this down to YouTube's appetite for apocalypse (the algorithm does love a cliff edge). The more interesting reading is that Hanson holds one worldview across both rooms, and the two framings show how much of the "AI risk" argument is a fight about feelings dressed as a fight about facts.

## The concession nobody noticed

Here is the line that should have ended the debate before it started. Hanson, in the interview, describing what he granted Eliezer Yudkowsky twenty years ago:

> "AI would eventually be more capable than non-AIs, and they would therefore kind of run the world… and then the rest of the world would be at their mercy. And that seems a pretty robust prediction for the future."

So the supposed optimist accepts the takeover. He accepts it the way he accepts weather. Industrialists replaced farmers; farmers replaced foragers; foragers replaced other primates. "You just don't expect that in the long run the new form comes to dominate" (he means you *should* expect it, and the transcript is merely being a transcript). What he rejects is Yudkowsky's version, the *foom*: one machine in a basement going from dim to omnipotent over a weekend, leaving no time to notice or bargain.

Krueger, in the debate, never quite lets the distinction land. His case keeps sliding from "we will lose control" to "and therefore everyone dies", as though dominance and extermination were the same event viewed at different zoom levels. For Hanson they belong to separate categories, and the difference between them is roughly the difference between a grandparent and a hostage.

## Descendants, technically

Hanson's reframe is the most useful thing in either video, and the debate barely gets to it.

The AI-risk community, he says, has cast AI as "this alien armada coming from another star." If you saw such a fleet a century out, of course you would arm yourself. Evolution built us to be wary of *coexisting rivals* (your brothers have long been suspicious of your cousins, and your cousins are hardly strangers). It also built us to be strangely indulgent of *descendants*, even very different ones. These are, he notes, two robust instincts pointing in opposite directions, and which one fires depends entirely on how the thing in front of you has been labelled.

AI, he argues, is plainly in the second category. It does not exist yet as a rival; if it ever becomes capable enough to be one, "it'll be because they're your descendants. We will make them, and they will then arise from us."

And he means *descendant* in the technical Darwinian sense, which Darwin worked out without knowing anything about DNA: something in the future that shares features with you because you caused it to. Humanity's whole trick for the last million years has been a second channel of inheritance, cultural evolution, in which we pass things on by being copied rather than by breeding. Models trained on the accumulated expression of human culture are a third channel. "I don't think you should be too squeamish about a new way to pass on features to descendants. That's what humanity has been all about."

Then comes the uncomfortable mirror. Your ancestors of a few thousand years ago would be impressed by your plumbing and appalled by you. You have betrayed their gods, abandoned their rites, thrown out most of what they thought made a person decent. They might hesitate to claim you. You, however, claim them without a second thought. Run that forward and the strange creatures of the future will feel entirely continuous with us, whether or not we return the favour.

Seen this way, the question "will AI replace us?" starts to sound like asking whether the Industrial Revolution replaced the medieval peasantry. It did, in the sense that matters to census-takers. Their descendants are reading this.

## Where the optimist keeps his fear

None of this makes Hanson cheerful, and the interview is where he says what actually frightens him.

His biggest worry about the future is **lock-in**: a world system "competent enough to enforce some strong world rules", one that never grows much larger and never quite collapses either. A civilisation that stops evolving because it has arranged, very sensibly, to stop anything from changing it.

He has a second worry, and it sharpens the first. Cultural evolution, our superpower, needs four things to work: many variants, real selection pressure, an environment that does not move too fast, and variants that do not wiggle too much on their own. Three centuries ago we had hundreds of thousands of small, poor, conservative peasant cultures, culled regularly by famine and war. Now we have one rich, peaceful, global elite monoculture, doubling its economy every twenty years and treating cultural activism as a virtue. Every dial has turned the wrong way. Falling fertility in conditions of peace and plenty is, for Hanson, the symptom that gives it away.

Hold that next to the debate and something odd appears. Krueger's endgame for AI-enabled bioweapons, by his own account, is "a totalitarian surveillance state" (which he says he is not keen on). His proposed remedy is to halt frontier AI development, a measure that could only be enforced with global monitoring of compute and chips. Hanson's nightmare is precisely the competent world authority that such a halt would need.

The two men fear the same Leviathan. One thinks unchecked AI will summon it; the other thinks the effort to check AI *is* the summoning.

There is a second irony, and it cuts against Hanson too. By his own theory, a single population of models trained on one global corpus is the purest monoculture ever produced, and he concedes as much in the interview ("too little variety"). If Krueger wanted a better argument than adversarial examples on cat photos, he could have borrowed Hanson's.

## The retirees

The passage that should be quoted more often is Hanson's thought experiment about pensioners.

Why don't we kill all the retirees and take their stuff? Most of us don't personally care about most retirees. The better explanation, he suggests, is that we share structures (law and finance, mostly) and the moment anyone breaks them to grab from one group, everyone else starts wondering who is next. Musical chairs, with knives.

If AIs grow up inside those shared institutions, the same logic should restrain them. If they live in "a separate world of finance and law and governance… on separate islands", it might not.

This is an alignment proposal. It simply refuses to be about values. Krueger's version of safety lives inside the model: what does it want, can we read its mind, will it generalise out of distribution? Hanson's version lives between agents: property rights, precedent, the shared fear of being the next 5% to be expropriated.

It is also, if you sit with it, quietly bleak. In this picture humanity is the retiree. Our safety rests on our descendants finding it too destabilising to rob us. (There are worse retirement plans. Most of them are currently on offer.)

## The matter of allegiance

Which brings us to the part of the debate that grates.

Near the end, Hanson makes his sharpest charge: the AI-risk world is insular. He was there for the original debates with Yudkowsky on *Overcoming Bias*; he was, in his telling, pushed out for disagreeing; and for twenty years the community has mostly been talking to itself. Contrarian clubs tend to look like this from outside. They have detailed arguments, they rehearse them to each other, and they are usually wrong.

Krueger's reply:

> "I think this is an ad hominem and you're mischaracterizing who I am. I'm primarily a machine learning researcher… I arrived at those concerns independently and have engaged with the AI safety community because those are other people who share those concerns."

Read on its own, that is a fair defence. He has said he entered the field around 2013, and there is no reason to doubt he formed his worries before anyone paid him to have them.

But "engaged with the community" is doing a lot of work. The public grant records show:

- **Open Philanthropy** (the largest funder in effective altruism, and the dominant patron of AI-existential-risk research) recommended **$250,000** over four years in 2021 to support Krueger's machine-learning research at Cambridge, under its "potential risks from advanced AI" programme. A further **$140,050** went through the Berkeley Existential Risk Initiative to support collaboration with him, and a smaller grant went to one of his collaborators.
- The **Long-Term Future Fund**, run by EA Funds, gave up to **$200,000** in May 2021 for PhD students and compute at his new Cambridge safety lab. The fund's own write-up says why: a new academic lab focused on AI safety could have "strong field-building effects."
- His CV, by his own account, adds a **$1,000,000** Open Philanthropy award in 2022 and **$880,000** from the Survival and Flourishing Fund, another longtermist funder. (These come from his CV; I have not matched them against the funders' own records.)

None of this is secret, and none of it is scandalous on its own. Researchers have to be paid by someone. The trouble lies in the posture. When accused of belonging to an insular ecosystem, Krueger described himself as an independent scientist who chats with it. The ecosystem's own paperwork describes him as a project it funded *specifically to grow the field*.

Two things follow, and neither requires questioning his sincerity.

The first is **disclosure**. In a debate that turned repeatedly on whose expertise counts (Hinton and Bengio on one side, economists and market prices on the other), the financial relationship between a speaker and the movement he is defending belongs on the table. He left it off.

There is also the matter of **selection**, which sits underneath both. Philanthropic money of this kind does not need to buy anyone's conclusions. It identifies people who already hold them, gives them students and GPUs, and lets the students graduate holding them too. When Krueger offers, as evidence, that "more and more people share these concerns", he is partly citing the output of a pipeline that was built, in writing, to produce exactly that trend.

The second is **symmetry**, and it is the one that stings. Krueger's whole case against the labs rests on incentives. Firms have a profit motive to underweight risk, he argues, and so they impose an externality on everyone. Fair enough. A lab whose existence depends on the risk being taken seriously has the opposite incentive, and the same scepticism applies to it. Krueger asks the audience to discount the people paid to build AI. He does not ask them to discount the people paid to fear it. That is the hypocrisy, and it does not need any exaggeration to be visible.

(Hanson, for the record, is a tenured economist whose priors about markets are no secret. One of the two men named his allegiances in the debate. The other declined to.)

## What Krueger gets right

It would be convenient to stop there. It would also be the sort of one-sided brief this piece is complaining about.

Krueger lands two blows that Hanson never parries. The first is on externalities. Hanson waves away the polling ("people don't like oil companies either"), which is fair as far as it goes, since dislike alone is no externality. Krueger's actual claim is a different and textbook one: a probabilistic, uncompensated cost imposed on third parties. Hanson never answers it.

The second is the **cyber/bio asymmetry**. AI that finds a software vulnerability can also patch it; there is no comparable defensive story for an engineered pathogen. Hanson's "why can't AI do biodefence too?" is a fair question, and the debate ends without either of them supplying a mechanism.

And on the deepest point, the warning-shot standard, Hanson concedes the limit himself. If a catastrophe has no smaller precursors, "you can't have a track record about that." The whole dispute over bioweapons comes down to whether COVID was a draw from a known distribution or a jump that should have been visible from the inside. Neither of them settles it, and it may not be settleable in a ninety-minute video.

## Evolution doing its thing

Strip away the thumbnails and what remains is a disagreement about kinship.

If AI is an armada, then halting it is self-defence, and the surveillance architecture required to enforce the halt is regrettable but necessary. If AI is a descendant, then halting it starts to look like the stable, competent, growth-blocking world system that Hanson considers the most plausible Great Filter we have left: the thing that explains the empty sky.

Hanson's position is easy to mishear as indifference to human survival. It reads better as a refusal to let fear of our children become the instrument that freezes us in place. He wants the takeover to be gradual, absorbed into shared law and shared property, slow enough that some of us can do what earlier generations did at every transition: send some of the family off to join the winners and buy into the new assets.

That advice has a social-performance problem, of course. "Join the winners" has always been easier for those who already own something to bring. The subsistence farmers of the Industrial Revolution were told, in effect, *we can make food other ways*. Hanson's own phrase for their fate is being "shunted aside because you're in the way." That deserves its own essay, and it is the part of his worldview that anyone who has watched a livelihood-restoration plan meet a construction schedule should find hardest to shrug at.

But on the narrow question these two videos pose, the economist under the doomer's title has the more coherent account. He has also been more honest about whose side he is on.

The children were always going to be strange. The question is whether we raise them, or pay someone to warn us about them.
